## YouTube Playlist Structure System for TotalRecalls

A well-built playlist system does two jobs at once. It helps viewers navigate your channel like a guided path, and it tells YouTube exactly what your channel is about. For a Windows desktop app that people need to see before they trust it, that structure matters even more.
This document gives you a complete playlist framework: the playlists to build, how to name and describe them, how to sort your first 10 videos into them, and how to use playlists to pull in search traffic. Everything here ties directly to your existing 10-video content plan and upload checklist, so the pieces snap together without extra work.
How this system works
Think of your channel as a library with clearly labeled shelves. Each playlist is a shelf built around one job: welcoming newcomers, proving the product works, capturing provider searches, answering doubts, or reinforcing your core promise.
Six playlists cover everything you need at launch. They map cleanly to the funnel — awareness, proof, search capture, objection handling, and conversion — and they leave room to grow as you add more videos.
Takeaway: Six focused playlists give viewers a clear path and give YouTube clear signals.

Naming conventions for consistency
Consistent names help viewers scan your channel and help YouTube group related content. Follow these rules for every playlist and video.
Playlist naming rules
Lead with the benefit or action, not your brand. "Back Up ChatGPT Conversations" beats "TotalRecalls ChatGPT Videos."
Keep provider playlists in one pattern: Back Up [Provider] [Conversations/Chat History]. This makes the whole set instantly recognizable.
Stay under ~50 characters so names don't truncate on mobile.
Use title case across all playlists for a clean, uniform look.
Video naming rules (already aligned with your content plan)
Put the primary keyword near the front — "Export ChatGPT Conversations…," "Back Up Claude Chat History…"
Match the video title to its home playlist's theme so the grouping feels natural.
Avoid clever wordplay in search-focused videos; write what people actually type.
A simple pattern to reuse
For every new provider you support, create one playlist and at least one video using these templates:
Playlist: Back Up [Provider] Chat History
Video: How to Save [Provider] Conversations Locally (Markdown + JSON)
Takeaway: One naming pattern across providers turns eight scattered videos into one recognizable set.
Using playlists for SEO and discoverability
Playlists are searchable on their own, and they can rank in both YouTube and Google search. Treat each one as a mini landing page.
Optimize the playlist itself
Write a keyword-rich description (see the six above) — the first sentence matters most, since it shows in previews.
Name the playlist around a real search phrase people use, like "back up ChatGPT conversations."
Set a strong first video. The opening video's thumbnail often represents the whole playlist, so lead with your clearest, most clickable piece.
Link playlists everywhere
Pin the "Start Here" playlist to your channel homepage as the featured section for new visitors.
Add playlist links in video descriptions using the "More from TotalRecalls" block already in your upload template.
Use end screens to send viewers into the matching playlist, not just the next single video.
Let playlists feed watch time
When someone finishes one video, autoplay pulls up the next in the playlist. Ordering related videos together keeps viewers watching, and longer session time signals quality to YouTube.
Takeaway: A well-named, well-described playlist can rank and pull in viewers on its own.

Growing the system over time
This structure scales cleanly. As you publish more, follow these simple rules.
Add a provider playlist each time you release that provider's first video. Reuse the naming pattern.
Split large playlists only when they pass ~15 videos, so viewers aren't overwhelmed.
Retire or merge playlists that never fill up, keeping the channel tidy.
Refresh descriptions whenever pricing or provider support changes, so nothing reads as outdated.
Takeaway: Keep new videos flowing into the existing pattern instead of inventing new categories.

Why playlists matter for a desktop utility

Naming conventions that scale
A consistent naming system keeps the channel tidy as you pass 50 or 100 videos. It also helps viewers scan quickly.
Playlist names
Use this pattern: [Topic or provider] + [outcome or format]
✅ "ChatGPT Backup Guides"
✅ "Privacy, Safety & Local-First Explained"
❌ "Stuff About Privacy"
❌ "TotalRecalls Videos Part 2"
Keep names short enough to read in full on mobile. Put the searchable word, usually the provider or the problem, first.
Video titles
Use this pattern: [Action or outcome] + [provider/keyword] + [format hint if useful]
"How to Export ChatGPT Conversations to Markdown"
"Back Up Your Claude Chat History Locally"
"Is TotalRecalls Safe? 100% Local, Here's the Proof"
Internal file names
Viewers never see these, but a solo founder benefits from them. Use TR-[number]-[type]-[topic], for example:
TR-04-LONG-chatgpt-export
TR-06-SHORT-provider-friction
This makes it easy to match raw footage, thumbnails, and Shorts cutdowns to their parent video.
Takeaway: Put the searchable word first for viewers and use numbered codes for yourself.
How to order videos within playlists
The order in a playlist is a quiet sales sequence. Here's what most channels get wrong: they default to "newest first," which puts the least-proven video at the top.
Follow these rules instead:
Set the order manually. Don't rely on date order.
Open with your strongest hook. The first video decides whether autoplay continues.
Move from problem to proof to action. Pain, then demo, then trust, then the Free Tier.
End on a conversion-ready video. Free vs Pro or the explainer makes a natural finale.
Review monthly. If one video holds viewers noticeably better, move it higher.
Takeaway: Arrange each playlist as a deliberate path, not an upload log.
How playlists help YouTube SEO
Playlists give YouTube more context about your channel. When related videos share a well-titled playlist, YouTube gets a clearer picture of what the channel covers. That can help your videos appear in search results and suggested feeds for related terms.
To get the most out of that:
Use keywords in playlist titles and descriptions. "ChatGPT Backup Guides" matches real searches better than a clever label.
Keep playlists focused. A tight topic sends a clearer signal than a catch-all.
Link playlists everywhere. Add them to end screens, pinned comments, video descriptions, and blog embeds.
Feature key playlists on your homepage. Put "Start Here" first, then "Product Demos," then your top provider guide.
Takeaway: Focused, keyword-led playlists help both people and the algorithm find you.
Common mistakes to avoid
Too many playlists too early. Seven is plenty at launch. Empty or near-empty shelves look abandoned.
Leaving out the CTA. Every playlist description should point to the site with the desktop note.
Stale urgency content. Remove launch-price videos from "Start Here" once the discount ends.
Inconsistent names. Mixing "Backup," "Back Up," and "Backups" looks careless. Pick one term and use it everywhere.
