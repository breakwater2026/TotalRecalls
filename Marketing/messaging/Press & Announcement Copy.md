Why AI conversations disappear
Chat platforms weren't designed to be permanent archives. They're built to answer your question right now — not to guarantee you can find that answer six months from today.
That gap creates real risk. Here's how it plays out:
Sessions expire. You come back to finish a research thread and find the trail wiped out.
Interfaces change. A UI update ships overnight and orphans threads you assumed were safe.
Downloads fail. The official export breaks halfway through, and an entire conversation vanishes.
Tabs close. One accidental click ends a debugging session you needed the next morning.
Nothing gets backed up. Nine months of prompts sit in one account, with no copy anywhere else.
None of these come with a warning. One day the conversation is there, the next it isn't — and there's no undo button.
The problem gets worse with every tool you add

## Blog Post

Why Your AI Conversations Deserve a Permanent Home
Somewhere on a server you don't control, there's a version of you that's had thousands of conversations. You've asked ChatGPT to debug your code, had Claude draft a contract, sent Perplexity down a research rabbit hole, and let Gemini summarize a stack of PDFs. Every one of those exchanges represents real time and real thinking — and almost none of it is actually yours to keep.
That's not a hypothetical risk. It's how these platforms are built.
The Quiet Fragility of Chat History
AI providers don't design their products around long-term archiving. They're optimized for the next conversation, not the one from six months ago. Which means your history is exposed to a handful of everyday failures:
UI redesigns that reorganize or hide old threads
Session expirations that quietly end a research trail
Download failures on export tools that were never built to be reliable
Takeout dumps — like Google's — that arrive as unreadable, unstructured archives
Closed tabs that take a half-finished debugging session with them
None of these require a data breach or an outage. They're just the ordinary friction of using cloud software, and they add up. Ask anyone who's tried to find a six-month-old ChatGPT conversation and watched the search come back empty.
Why "It's in the Cloud" Isn't the Same as "It's Safe"
There's a comforting assumption that if something lives in the cloud, it's backed up and permanent. In practice, the opposite is often true. Cloud-hosted chat history exists at the discretion of the provider — subject to their retention policies, their infrastructure choices, and their product roadmap. You don't own the file. You have access to a view of it, for as long as the provider decides to keep offering that view.
If you use one AI assistant, that's a manageable risk. If you use several — which is increasingly the norm — the risk multiplies. Your most valuable thinking is spread across separate accounts, each with its own quirks, its own export limitations, and its own timeline for change.
What Ownership Actually Looks Like
Real ownership means the conversation lives on a device you control, in a format you can open without anyone's permission. That's the entire premise behind TotalRecalls, a Windows desktop app built to solve exactly this problem.
TotalRecalls connects to eight major AI providers — ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, Gemini, Grok, DeepSeek, Mistral, and Qwen — and downloads your conversations directly to a folder on your own PC. Each conversation is saved as a plain Markdown file (with a companion JSON file for structured data), which means:
You can open it in any text editor, not just a proprietary viewer
You can search it, back it up, or move it to another machine without a login
It doesn't disappear if a provider changes its export policy tomorrow
Nothing gets uploaded anywhere in the process. The app runs entirely locally — your conversations never leave your machine, and there's no account to create in order to use it.
One App, Instead of Eight Different Habits
The other problem TotalRecalls solves is more practical than existential: keeping track of eight different export processes is exhausting. Each provider handles data downloads differently — Gemini requires parsing a Google Takeout archive, Perplexity's research threads expire, and Grok conversations reference X posts that complicate a clean export.
TotalRecalls standardizes all of it. Pick a provider, connect your account locally, choose a destination folder, and download. The result is a single library — organized by provider, searchable, and consistent — instead of eight scattered habits you have to remember to maintain.
